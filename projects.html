Projects and collaborations

Current and past collaborations in molecular ecology and biodiversity research, from Hawaiian field surveys to natural DNA samplers and biological archives.

Selected projects

DFG Walter Benjamin Fellowship, UC Berkeley, 2025-2026

Long-term biodiversity change in Hawaiʻi

This project investigates decade-scale biodiversity change across the Hawaiian archipelago, with a focus on arthropods, plant-arthropod interaction networks, and ecological change through time.

The work combines field sampling, molecular biodiversity monitoring, archived biological material, and ecological interpretation to ask how communities shift across space and time.

Related project: Hawaiʻi Dimensions of Biodiversity (Rosemary Gillespie) — Berkeley project announcement.

UC Berkeley, 2023-2024

Early detection of invasive arthropods

As part of the EDIAs project, I worked on eDNA-based methods for biodiversity monitoring and early detection of invasive arthropods.

This research includes fieldwork in Hawaiʻi, reference database development for Hawaiian taxa, and molecular workflows that can help detect ecological change before it is obvious through conventional surveys.

PhD research, Trier University, 2020-2023

Natural eDNA samplers for retrospective biomonitoring

My doctoral work tested how organisms and biological material can act as passive collectors of biodiversity information. This included spiders, mussels and other filter feeders, leaves, and museum-derived material such as herbarium specimens.

These approaches make it possible to reconstruct communities and interactions from samples that were not originally collected for modern molecular biodiversity research.

Trier University · Environmental Specimen Bank

Biodiversity change in the German Environmental Specimen Bank

With colleagues at Trier University, I contributed to research using archived material from the German Environmental Specimen Bank (Umweltprobenbank) to reconstruct biodiversity change. Samples originally collected under standardized protocols for pollution monitoring preserve DNA from associated organisms, opening a window into past communities.

Our 2022 study used archived leaves to trace forest arthropod communities over decades. It found gradual community turnover and increasing similarity among communities, while local species richness showed no widespread decline. A 2025 collaborative study extended this approach across four decades and terrestrial, freshwater, and marine systems using archived leaves, macroalgae, and mussels. These studies show how established biological archives can support long-term molecular biodiversity assessment.

Trier University and Bayer AG, 2023

eDNA-based pollinator monitoring

This collaboration focused on designing workflows to detect changes in pollinator communities and developing an academic-industry cooperation around eDNA-based monitoring.

The broader goal is to make molecular tools useful for conservation-facing biodiversity assessment, where methods need to be practical, repeatable, and interpretable.
